Lecture 11

Part of a pair: reduced states and purification

The two spins of hydrogen in their ground state at zero field, ψ−\psi^-ψ−, are entangled, so the electron alone is in no pure state. This lecture finds the density matrix that describes one part of a pair. The electron of ψ−\psi^-ψ− turns out to be the unpolarised beam of Lecture 3, and no operation on the proton changes the electron’s statistics unless its outcome is recorded and used. Conversely, every density matrix is the state of one part of a pure pair. The entropy of a part measures how entangled a pure pair is. A system at a temperature has a mixed state, the thermal state. The lecture ends with the one place where the course leaves ideal measurement: a probe read ideally can give a reading of the system that is not ideal.

11.2 A pure pair with mixed parts

Proposition 11.3 (Entangled pairs have mixed parts). A unit vector ψ\psiψ of the pair is a product if and only if ρ1\rho_1ρ1​ is pure, and if and only if ρ2\rho_2ρ2​ is pure.

Proof. For every vector vvv, ⟨v,CC†v⟩=∥C†v∥2\inner v{C\adj Cv}=\norm{\adj Cv}^2⟨v,CC†v⟩=​C†v​2. So CC†v=0C\adj Cv=0CC†v=0 exactly when C†v=0\adj Cv=0C†v=0, the two matrices have the same kernel, and rank⁡ρ1=rank⁡C†=rank⁡C\rank\rho_1=\rank\adj C=\rank Crankρ1​=rankC†=rankC. A density matrix is pure exactly when its rank is 111. Indeed, a density matrix of rank 111 is λ∣u⟩ ⁣⟨u∣\lambda\proj uλ∣u⟩⟨u∣ with a unit vector uuu, and its trace gives λ=1\lambda=1λ=1. By Theorem 10.4, ψ\psiψ is a product exactly when rank⁡C=1\rank C=1rankC=1. The same argument with ρ2=CTC‾\rho_2=C^{\mathsf T}\overline Cρ2​=CTC and rank⁡C‾=rank⁡C\rank\overline C=\rank CrankC=rankC gives the second statement. 

For the ground state ψ−\psi^-ψ− of hydrogen’s two spins at zero field, C=12(01−10)C=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)C=2​1​(0−1​10​), and ρ1=ρ2=1211.\rho_1=\rho_2=\tfrac12\Id .ρ1​=ρ2​=21​11. The electron alone is unpolarised. A reading of its spin along any axis gives ±1\pm1±1 with probability 12\tfrac1221​ each. Its density matrix is that of the beam of silver atoms straight from the oven in Lecture 3. By Theorem 11.1 and Theorem 3.4, no experiment on the electron alone, and no sequence of experiments, tells the two apart.

Yet the two were prepared in different ways. The oven beam is an ensemble. Each atom is in some pure state, and we do not know which. The electron of ψ−\psi^-ψ− belongs to a pair that is in a pure state, and the electron alone is in none. A mixture that arises as one part of a pure pair is called an improper mixture, after d’Espagnat. What this difference means, if anything, is a question of interpretation, taken up in Quantum Mechanics III: Foundations. The predictions are the same.

11.3 How entangled: Schmidt’s form and the entropy

How mixed the part of a pure pair is measures how entangled the pair is.

11.4 What the other system can and cannot change

Can something done to the proton change what a reading of the electron gives?

Proposition 11.8 (No signalling). For every density matrix ρ\rhoρ of the pair, the reduced density matrix ρ1\rho_1ρ1​ is unchanged by a unitary 11⊗V\Id\otimes V11⊗V, and by a reading of an observable 11⊗B\Id\otimes B11⊗B of the second system whose reading is not recorded.

Proof. For the unitary, Tr⁡((11⊗V)ρ (11⊗V†)(A⊗11))=Tr⁡(ρ (A⊗11))\Tr\bigl((\Id\otimes V)\rho\,(\Id\otimes\adj V)(A\otimes\Id)\bigr) =\Tr\bigl(\rho\,(A\otimes\Id)\bigr)Tr((11⊗V)ρ(11⊗V†)(A⊗11))=Tr(ρ(A⊗11)), since 11⊗V†\Id\otimes\adj V11⊗V† commutes with A⊗11A\otimes\IdA⊗11 and the trace is cyclic. For the reading, the observables A⊗11A\otimes\IdA⊗11 and 11⊗B\Id\otimes B11⊗B commute, and by the fourth condition of Theorem 5.2 a reading of 11⊗B\Id\otimes B11⊗B that is not recorded does not change the statistics of A⊗11A\otimes\IdA⊗11. These statistics, for every AAA, fix ρ1\rho_1ρ1​. 

An interaction is not of this kind, and it can change ρ1\rho_1ρ1​. In the evolution of Example 10.6, which starts from ∣01⟩\ket{01}∣01⟩, (11.2) gives ρ1(t)=diag(cos⁡2(νt/2),sin⁡2(νt/2))\rho_1(t)=\mathrm{diag}\bigl(\cos^2(\freq t/2),\sin^2(\freq t/2)\bigr)ρ1​(t)=diag(cos2(νt/2),sin2(νt/2)). The electron’s Bloch vector (0,0,cos⁡νt)(0,0,\cos\freq t)(0,0,cosνt) passes through the centre of the ball at νt=π/2\freq t=\pi/2νt=π/2.

A reading of the second system that is recorded can change what we know about the first.

It shows when the electron’s readings are sorted by the proton’s readings, or in a reading of the pair together.

11.5 Every mixture is part of a pure pair

Lecture 3 met density matrices as ensembles. This lecture met them as parts of pure pairs. The two sources give the same objects.

Theorem 11.10 (Purification). Let ρ\rhoρ be a density matrix on Cn\C^nCn. The vector ψρ\psi_\rhoψρ​ of Cn⊗Cn\C^n\otimes\C^nCn⊗Cn with the coefficient matrix C=ρC=\sqrt\rhoC=ρ​, the positive square root of ρ\rhoρ, is a unit vector with ρ1=ρ,ρ2=ρT.\rho_1=\rho,\qquad \rho_2=\rho^{\mathsf T} .ρ1​=ρ,ρ2​=ρT. 11.7 A measurement that is not ideal

Every measurement so far has been ideal. A real detector reads a system through a probe coupled to it. Here the probe is read ideally, and the measurement it makes of the system is not ideal.

The part: Tr⁡(ρ1A)=Tr⁡(ρ (A⊗11))\Tr(\rho_1A)=\Tr\bigl(\rho\,(A\otimes\Id)\bigr)Tr(ρ1​A)=Tr(ρ(A⊗11)); for a pure state ρ1=CC†\rho_1=C\adj Cρ1​=CC†, ρ2=CTC‾\rho_2=C^{\mathsf T}\overline Cρ2​=CTC (Theorem 11.1). Summary

  • The state of a part: every experiment on one part is predicted by its reduced density matrix (Theorem 11.1).
  • No signalling: nothing done to the second system, without a record, changes the first system’s statistics (Proposition 11.8).
  • Purification: every density matrix is the state of one part of a pure pair (Theorem 11.10).
  • How entangled: the parts of a pure pair have one entropy, its measure of entanglement (Theorem 11.4).
