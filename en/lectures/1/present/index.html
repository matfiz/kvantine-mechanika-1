Lecture 1

The qubit

A quantum system is given by its states, its observables, a Hamiltonian that moves the states, and a rule for the probabilities of readings and the state after a reading. This lecture builds all four for the simplest system, a quantum bit, starting from silver atoms in a magnet. The states are the points of a sphere, and the Hamiltonian turns the sphere.

1.1 Silver atoms and two magnets

Silver atoms from an oven pass through a Stern–Gerlach magnet, whose field is strong at one pole and weak at the other, and land on a screen. The uneven field pushes on the atom’s magnetic moment, the vector that measures how the atom responds to a magnetic field. The screen shows two separated beams (Figure 1.1; Sakurai and Napolitano, Figs 1.1–1.2). They correspond to the two readings of the atom’s intrinsic angular momentum, its spin, along the magnet’s direction. This lecture is the theory of the spin alone. We record the two readings as +1+1+1 and −1-1−1.

Keep only the atoms that read +1+1+1 in a magnet along zzz. Between the magnets the atoms fly through no field. Three facts describe what happens next (Figure 1.2), and this lecture will explain them.

(F1) A repeated reading repeats.

A second magnet along zzz reads +1+1+1 for all of them.

(F2) Probabilities vary smoothly.

A second magnet turned through an angle θ\thetaθ still has only two possible readings, +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. The fraction of atoms reading +1+1+1 is cos⁡2(θ/2)\cos^2(\theta/2)cos2(θ/2), and the fraction reading −1-1−1 is sin⁡2(θ/2)\sin^2(\theta/2)sin2(θ/2).

(F3) A reading changes the state.

Keep the atoms that read +1+1+1 along xxx (θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2), and send them through a magnet along zzz. Half of them now read −1-1−1.

These fractions are the limits that measured frequencies approach.

No three-dimensional vector with three definite components behaves like this. Its components would not take only two values, and a reading along xxx could not erase a reading along zzz. We need a state that holds the probabilities for every direction. We need rules that give those probabilities and the state after a reading, and a rule for the motion of a state in a field. Atoms straight from the oven, before any magnet, are taken up in Lecture 3.

1.4 Observables and measurement

Postulate 1.5 (Observables). Each measurable quantity of a qubit, an observable, is a self-adjoint matrix A=A†A=\adj AA=A† on C2\C^2C2. Its possible readings are its eigenvalues.

Physicists also say Hermitian. This course says self-adjoint. By the spectral theorem (Linear Algebra for Physics, from now on Linear Algebra, Thm 10.17), a self-adjoint AAA is A=∑aa Ea,A=\sum_aa\,E_a,A=a∑​aEa​, where aaa runs over the distinct eigenvalues of AAA, and EaE_aEa​ is the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace ker⁡(A−a11)\ker(A-a\Id)ker(A−a11) of aaa. For a self-adjoint matrix, ∑aEa=11\sum_aE_a=\Id∑a​Ea​=11.

Postulate 1.6 (Measurement). An ideal measurement of an observable AAA on the state [ψ][\psi][ψ] obeys two rules.

  1. Born’s rule. It gives the reading aaa with probability Prob⁡(a)=⟨ψ,Eaψ⟩=∥Eaψ∥2.\Prob(a)=\inner{\psi}{E_a\psi}=\norm{E_a\psi}^2 .Prob(a)=⟨ψ,Ea​ψ⟩=∥Ea​ψ∥2.

  2. Lüders’ rule. If the reading is aaa, which requires Prob⁡(a)>0\Prob(a)>0Prob(a)>0, the state immediately afterwards is the ray [Eaψ∥Eaψ∥].\Bigl[\frac{E_a\psi}{\norm{E_a\psi}}\Bigr].[∥Ea​ψ∥Ea​ψ​].

Both rules depend only on the ray, since a phase in front of ψ\psiψ changes neither the probability ∥Eaψ∥2\norm{E_a\psi}^2∥Ea​ψ∥2 nor the ray of EaψE_a\psiEa​ψ. They are two separate assumptions, and Lüders’ rule does not follow from Born’s rule. The change of state is often called collapse. The postulate predicts what follows a reading, which (F1) and (F3) require. It claims nothing about how, or whether, a physical process brings the change about. Measurements that are not ideal are studied in Quantum Mechanics III: Foundations. A reading of probability 000 does not occur, and it has no state after it.

Prepare the state ψ(θ₀, φ₀), choose the axis n of the Hamiltonian H = c + (ħν/2) n·σ and run the clock: r turns about n on the circle where n·r stays fixed, anticlockwise seen from the tip of n (Theorem 1.12).     1.7 Is the Bloch sphere a phase space?

    In classical mechanics the state of a system is a point (q,p)(q,p)(q,p) of its phase space, and the energy, a function h(q,p)h(q,p)h(q,p), moves the points by Hamilton’s equations q˙=∂h/∂p\dot q=\partial h/\partial pq˙​=∂h/∂p and p˙=−∂h/∂q\dot p=-\partial h/\partial qp˙​=−∂h/∂q. Take n=ez\nvec=\mathbf e_zn=ez​. Away from the poles, use the local coordinates q=ϕq=\phiq=ϕ and p=ℏ2cos⁡θp=\tfrac\hbar2\cos\thetap=2ℏ​cosθ on the sphere, and the energy function h=c+ℏν2cos⁡θ=c+νph=c+\tfrac{\hbar\freq}{2}\cos\theta=c+\freq ph=c+2ℏν​cosθ=c+νp. Then q˙=ν\dot q=\freqq˙​=ν and p˙=0\dot p=0p˙​=0: the azimuth grows at the rate ν\freqν at a fixed height, which is the precession about ez\mathbf e_zez​. The poles, where the coordinates fail, are the stationary states. Exercise Sheet 1 does the same for every axis. For the motion, then, the sphere is a phase space.

    For the readings, it is not. On a classical phase space a point fixes the value of every observable. A point of the Bloch sphere fixes the reading along m\mvecm only when m=±r\mvec=\pm\blochvm=±r, and along any other axis only its probabilities. A quantum state moves like a point of a phase space, but it holds probabilities, not values.

    Summary

    • The Bloch sphere: states are points, and orthogonal states are antipodal (Theorem 1.3).
    • Born’s rule on the sphere and precession: the statistics of a measurement depend on one component, and a Hamiltonian turns the sphere (Corollary 1.8 and Theorem 1.12). A point of the sphere holds probabilities, not values.
