Lecture 8

A particle on a ring of NNN sites

What this lecture does

A particle that can sit on any of NNN sites arranged in a ring, and hop between neighbouring sites, is the first system in this course with a position. Lecture 6 showed from its symmetries alone that it has a degenerate level. This lecture finds all its levels. The shift that moves the particle round the ring is diagonal in the basis of waves around the ring, the discrete Fourier basis, and so is the Hamiltonian. Benzene, in a simple model, is the ring of six sites. A particle started on one site spreads round the ring and, for six sites, comes back. With two sites in each cell and unequal alternating hops, the levels form two bands separated by a gap.

8.1 A particle that hops

Take N≥3N\ge3N≥3 sites on a ring, labelled x∈ZNx\in\Z_Nx∈ZN​, so that the site x+Nx+Nx+N is the site xxx. A particle sits on one of them, and the state in which it sits on the site xxx is the unit vector exe_xex​. The vectors exe_xex​ are an orthonormal basis of CN\C^NCN, and a measurement in this basis tells which site the particle is on. If the particle could not move, every exe_xex​ would have the same energy ccc, the on-site energy. A particle that can hop to a neighbouring site has the Hamiltonian H=c11−κ(T+T†),κ>0,\begin{equation} \label{eq:8:H} H=c\Id-\kappa\bigl(T+\adj T\bigr),\qquad \kappa>0 , \end{equation}H=c11−κ(T+T†),κ>0,(8.1) where TTT is the shift of §6.7, Tex=ex+1Te_x=e_{x+1}Tex​=ex+1​, and T†=T−1\adj T=T^{-1}T†=T−1 shifts back. In the basis exe_xex​, the matrix of HHH has ccc on the diagonal, −κ-\kappa−κ in the entries that join neighbouring sites, and 000 elsewhere. The number κ\kappaκ, the hopping amplitude, sets how fast the particle moves. With −κ-\kappa−κ in front and κ>0\kappa>0κ>0, the state N−1/2∑xexN^{-1/2}\sum_xe_xN−1/2∑x​ex​, with equal amplitudes on all the sites, has the lowest energy, as §8.3 shows. Lecture 6 found two symmetries of HHH: the shift TTT and the mirror MMM, Mex=e−xMe_x=e_{-x}Mex​=e−x​. They do not commute, so HHH has a degenerate level (Theorem 6.5). Which level is it, and are there others?

8.2 The Fourier basis

A symmetry whose eigenvalues are all distinct would settle the question at once. By the argument in the proof of Lemma 4.3, HHH maps each eigenspace of such a symmetry into itself. Put ζ=e2πi/N\zeta=e^{2\pi\ii/N}ζ=e2πi/N, and for k∈ZNk\in\Z_Nk∈ZN​ let fk=1N∑x∈ZNζkxex.\begin{equation} \label{eq:8:fk} f_k=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{x\in\Z_N}\zeta^{kx}e_x . \end{equation}fk​=N​1​x∈ZN​∑​ζkxex​.(8.2) The amplitude of fkf_kfk​ on the site xxx is ζkx/N\zeta^{kx}/\sqrt Nζkx/N​. 8.4 Benzene

Example 8.2 (Benzene in the Hückel model). The benzene molecule is a ring of six carbon atoms. The model of Hückel treats each electron on its own, as a particle on the ring with one state for each carbon atom, an on-site energy ccc and a hopping amplitude κ\kappaκ between neighbours. Hops to farther atoms are neglected. This is (8.1) with N=6N=6N=6. The energy eigenstates of one electron are called its orbitals. By Theorem 8.1, the energies of the six orbitals are c−2κ (k=0),c−κ (k=±1),c+κ (k=±2),c+2κ (k=3),c-2\kappa\ (k=0),\qquad c-\kappa\ (k=\pm1),\qquad c+\kappa\ (k=\pm2),\qquad c+2\kappa\ (k=3),c−2κ (k=0),c−κ (k=±1),c+κ (k=±2),c+2κ (k=3), two simple levels and two degenerate pairs (Figure 8.1). The model gives the orbitals and their order, not the measured spectrum of the molecule.

The hexagon in Figure 8.1 is the construction of Frost and Musulin. In it, the vertex at the angle 2πk/62\pi k/62πk/6 from the bottom has the height c−2κcos⁡(2πk/6)c-2\kappa\cos(2\pi k/6)c−2κcos(2πk/6). For any NNN, the same holds with a regular NNN-gon.

8.6 A ring with a flux

For a charged particle, a magnetic field through the middle of the ring is described, in a simple model, by a phase on each hop. The mirror is no longer a symmetry, since MHθM=H−θMH_\theta M=H_{-\theta}MHθ​M=H−θ​, which differs from HθH_\thetaHθ​ unless θ\thetaθ is a multiple of π\piπ. The shift remains, and alone it forces nothing, as Lecture 6 noted after Theorem 6.5. At the special values θ=πr/N\theta=\pi r/Nθ=πr/N, for an integer rrr, a level is degenerate again, and another symmetry, a mirror combined with a phase on each site, appears (Exercise Sheet 8).

Hückel proposed the model of Example 8.2 in 1931 to explain the stability of benzene. The model keeps six electrons, one from each carbon atom, which chemists call the π\piπ electrons. In the lowest energy state of the molecule, in this model, they fill the three lowest orbitals, k=0k=0k=0 and k=±1k=\pm1k=±1, two electrons to an orbital. Why at most two is the subject of Lecture 14. Frost and Musulin gave the polygon construction of Figure 8.1 in 1953. A magnetic flux through a ring of metal about a micrometre across shifts the energies of its electrons as in §8.6. In a cold ring a small current can then circulate in equilibrium, with no battery. Such persistent currents have been measured in rings of aluminium (Bleszynski-Jayich et al., 2009). Hückel wrote the hopping as β\betaβ, with β<0\beta<0β<0. Here β=−κ\beta=-\kappaβ=−κ. In a long chain of carbon atoms with alternating single and double bonds, polyacetylene, the model of §8.7 gives a gap between two bands. Su, Schrieffer and Heeger used such a model in 1979. Doped with other atoms, the polymer conducts, and the Nobel prize in chemistry of 2000 went to Heeger, MacDiarmid and Shirakawa for conducting polymers.

Summary
