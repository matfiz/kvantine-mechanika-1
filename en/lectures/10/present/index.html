Lecture 10

Two systems: tensor products and entanglement

A hydrogen atom in its ground state carries two spins, the electron’s and the proton’s, and they interact. This lecture gives the state space of a pair of systems: the tensor product of the two state spaces. A vector of the pair is described by a matrix of coefficients. The pair’s state is a product of states of the parts exactly when this matrix has rank 111. Most states of a pair are not products, and such states are called entangled. The ground state of hydrogen’s two spins is entangled, and an interaction can entangle two spins that start as a product. As a magnetic field grows, the ground state approaches a product state.

10.1 Two systems at once

Parts I and II treated one system at a time. Take now two systems, with state spaces Cn\C^nCn and Cm\C^mCm: the spin of the electron and the spin of the proton in a hydrogen atom, say, each a qubit. What should the state space of the pair be? Three requirements come from experiment.

First, each system can be read alone. A reading of an observable AAA of the first system is still a reading, with the outcomes of AAA, when the second system is present.

Second, two systems prepared independently give independent readings. If the first is prepared in [u][u][u] and the second in [w][w][w], a reading aaa of the first and a reading bbb of the second should occur together with probability ⟨u,Eau⟩⟨w,Gbw⟩\inner u{E_au}\inner w{G_bw}⟨u,Ea​u⟩⟨w,Gb​w⟩, the product of the two probabilities, where EaE_aEa​ and GbG_bGb​ are the projections of the two readings.

Third, the number of outcomes fixes the dimension. Read a complete observable of each system, one with nnn distinct readings and one with mmm. The pair then has nmnmnm distinguishable joint outcomes. As in §4.1, an observable with nmnmnm distinct readings has nmnmnm orthonormal eigenvectors. So the pair’s state space has dimension at least nmnmnm.

The tensor product of Linear Algebra, Def. 12.2, meets all three. The space Cn⊗Cm\C^n\otimes\C^mCn⊗Cm has the basis ei⊗eje_i\otimes e_jei​⊗ej​, where eie_iei​ runs over the standard basis of Cn\C^nCn and eje_jej​ over that of Cm\C^mCm, so its dimension is nmnmnm. It has the inner product ⟨u⊗w,u′⊗w′⟩=⟨u,u′⟩⟨w,w′⟩\inner{u\otimes w}{u'\otimes w'}=\inner u{u'}\inner w{w'}⟨u⊗w,u′⊗w′⟩=⟨u,u′⟩⟨w,w′⟩ (Linear Algebra, Rem. 12.7), in which the ei⊗eje_i\otimes e_jei​⊗ej​ are orthonormal. For matrices AAA on Cn\C^nCn and BBB on Cm\C^mCm, the matrix A⊗BA\otimes BA⊗B acts by (A⊗B)(u⊗w)=Au⊗Bw(A\otimes B)(u\otimes w)=Au\otimes Bw(A⊗B)(u⊗w)=Au⊗Bw (Linear Algebra, Prop. 12.9). In particular the projection Ea⊗GbE_a\otimes G_bEa​⊗Gb​ gives ⟨u⊗w,(Ea⊗Gb)(u⊗w)⟩=⟨u,Eau⟩⟨w,Gbw⟩,\inner{u\otimes w}{(E_a\otimes G_b)(u\otimes w)} =\inner u{E_au}\inner w{G_bw} ,⟨u⊗w,(Ea​⊗Gb​)(u⊗w)⟩=⟨u,Ea​u⟩⟨w,Gb​w⟩, which is the second requirement.

Postulate 10.1 (Two systems).

  1. States. Two systems with the state spaces Cn\C^nCn and Cm\C^mCm form a system with the state space Cn⊗Cm\C^n\otimes\C^mCn⊗Cm.

  2. Local readings. A reading of an observable AAA of the first system alone is a reading of the observable A⊗11A\otimes\IdA⊗11 of the pair. A reading of an observable BBB of the second system alone is a reading of 11⊗B\Id\otimes B11⊗B.

  3. Independent preparations. Two systems prepared independently in the states [u][u][u] and [w][w][w] form the pair in the state [u⊗w][u\otimes w][u⊗w].

  4. No interaction. Two systems that do not interact have the Hamiltonian H1⊗11+11⊗H2H_1\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes H_2H1​⊗11+11⊗H2​, where H1H_1H1​ and H2H_2H2​ are their own Hamiltonians.

The three requirements motivate the postulate. They do not prove it, and it is a postulate, like those of Lecture 1 and Lecture 4. Everything else about the pair follows from those postulates applied to Cn⊗Cm\C^n\otimes\C^mCn⊗Cm.

Every vector of the pair is a combination of the basis vectors, ψ=∑i=1n∑j=1mCij ei⊗ej,\begin{equation} \label{eq:10:C} \psi=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^mC_{ij}\,e_i\otimes e_j , \end{equation}ψ=i=1∑n​j=1∑m​Cij​ei​⊗ej​,(10.1) and we call the n×mn\times mn×m matrix CCC its coefficient matrix. The norm is ∥ψ∥2=∑i,j∣Cij∣2=Tr⁡(C†C)\norm\psi^2=\sum_{i,j}\abs{C_{ij}}^2=\Tr(\adj CC)∥ψ∥2=∑i,j​∣Cij​∣2=Tr(C†C). A product has the coefficients Cij=uiwjC_{ij}=u_iw_jCij​=ui​wj​, that is, C=uwTC=uw^{\mathsf T}C=uwT, the column uuu times the row wTw^{\mathsf T}wT. For two qubits we write ∣jk⟩=∣j⟩⊗∣k⟩\ket{jk}=\ket j\otimes\ket k∣jk⟩=∣j⟩⊗∣k⟩, in the order ∣00⟩,∣01⟩,∣10⟩,∣11⟩\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}∣00⟩,∣01⟩,∣10⟩,∣11⟩, in which the label of the first factor changes slowest. With the electron first, ∣01⟩\ket{01}∣01⟩ is the electron up and the proton down.

10.2 Local readings and local evolution

The observables A⊗11A\otimes\IdA⊗11 and 11⊗B\Id\otimes B11⊗B commute, since both products equal A⊗BA\otimes BA⊗B by Linear Algebra, Prop. 12.9. By Theorem 5.2 they are compatible, and their joint reading has the projections Ea⊗GbE_a\otimes G_bEa​⊗Gb​. So a reading of each system can be made at once, in either order, with the same joint statistics. If the pair is in the product state [u⊗w][u\otimes w][u⊗w], the two readings are independent, by the computation before Postulate 10.1.

The coefficient matrix turns local operations into matrix products.

10.3 Product and entangled states

Definition 10.3 (Product and entangled states). A state [ψ][\psi][ψ] of the pair is a product state if ψ=u⊗w\psi=u\otimes wψ=u⊗w for some vectors u∈Cnu\in\C^nu∈Cn and w∈Cmw\in\C^mw∈Cm. Otherwise it is entangled.

A product state is a pair of states, one for each system: the ray of uuu and the ray of www. An entangled state is a state of the pair that does not come from a state of each part.

Theorem 10.4 (The rank test). A unit vector ψ\psiψ with the coefficient matrix CCC is a product u⊗wu\otimes wu⊗w if and only if rank⁡C=1\rank C=1rankC=1. For two qubits this holds if and only if det⁡C=0\det C=0detC=0.

Proof. If ψ=u⊗w\psi=u\otimes wψ=u⊗w, then C=uwTC=uw^{\mathsf T}C=uwT. Every column of CCC is a multiple of uuu, so rank⁡C≤1\rank C\le1rankC≤1, and C≠0C\ne0C=0 since ψ≠0\psi\ne0ψ=0. Hence rank⁡C=1\rank C=1rankC=1.

Conversely, let rank⁡C=1\rank C=1rankC=1. The columns of CCC span a line, spanned by some vector uuu, so the jjj-th column is wjuw_juwj​u for a number wjw_jwj​. Then Cij=uiwjC_{ij}=u_iw_jCij​=ui​wj​, and ψ=∑i,juiwj ei⊗ej=u⊗w\psi=\sum_{i,j}u_iw_j\,e_i\otimes e_j=u\otimes wψ=∑i,j​ui​wj​ei​⊗ej​=u⊗w.

For two qubits, CCC is a nonzero 2×22\times22×2 matrix, and its rank is 111 exactly when det⁡C=0\det C=0detC=0. 

Linear Algebra, Ex. 12.3 and Def. 17.11, previews this test. Nothing below depends on it. For two qubits the test is a single equation, C00C11=C01C10C_{00}C_{11}=C_{01}C_{10}C00​C11​=C01​C10​. A coefficient matrix chosen at random does not satisfy it. Most states of two qubits are entangled.

By Proposition 10.2, local unitaries U⊗VU\otimes VU⊗V do not change the rank of the coefficient matrix. So two systems that do not interact, and evolve by (10.2), stay a product if they start as one, and stay entangled if they start entangled. Under evolution, only an interaction between the two systems can entangle them. A reading of an observable of the pair, not of either part alone, can entangle them too (Exercise Sheet 10). §10.5 shows an interaction that entangles.

10.4 Correlation is not entanglement

The state ψ−=12(∣01⟩−∣10⟩)\psi^-=\frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(\ket{01}-\ket{10}\bigr)ψ−=2​1​(∣01⟩−∣10⟩) has the coefficient matrix C=12(01−10)C=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)C=2​1​(0−1​10​), with det⁡C=12≠0\det C=\tfrac12\ne0detC=21​=0, so, by Theorem 10.4, it is entangled. Read both spins along zzz. The joint projections are Es⊗EtE_s\otimes E_tEs​⊗Et​, s,t∈{+,−}s,t\in\{+,-\}s,t∈{+,−}, with E±=12(11±σz)E_\pm=\tfrac12(\Id\pm\sigma_z)E±​=21​(11±σz​), and the two readings are always opposite. The outcomes are ↑↓\uparrow\downarrow↑↓ and ↓↑\downarrow\uparrow↓↑, each with probability 12\tfrac1221​.

Opposite readings along zzz do not need entanglement. Take the ensemble of pairs {(12,∣01⟩),(12,∣10⟩)}\{(\tfrac12,\ket{01}),(\tfrac12,\ket{10})\}{(21​,∣01⟩),(21​,∣10⟩)}, prepared by tossing a coin and preparing the product ∣01⟩\ket{01}∣01⟩ or ∣10⟩\ket{10}∣10⟩. Its density matrix ρ=12(∣01⟩ ⁣⟨01∣+∣10⟩ ⁣⟨10∣)\rho=\tfrac12\bigl(\proj{01}+\proj{10}\bigr)ρ=21​(∣01⟩⟨01∣+∣10⟩⟨10∣) gives the same opposite readings along zzz, with the same probabilities. The two preparations differ along xxx. Since (σx⊗σx)ψ−=−ψ−(\sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x)\psi^-=-\psi^-(σx​⊗σx​)ψ−=−ψ−, the mean of the product of the two readings along xxx is −1-1−1 in ψ−\psi^-ψ−. So the readings along xxx are again opposite, with certainty. In the ensemble the mean is Tr⁡(ρ (σx⊗σx))=0\Tr\bigl(\rho\,(\sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x)\bigr)=0Tr(ρ(σx​⊗σx​))=0, and the two readings along xxx agree with probability 12\tfrac1221​.

No ensemble of product states gives opposite readings with certainty along both axes. For a product u⊗wu\otimes wu⊗w, with Bloch vectors r\blochvr of uuu and s\mathbf ss of www, the means of the products along zzz and along xxx add up to ⟨u⊗w,(σz⊗σz+σx⊗σx) u⊗w⟩=rzsz+rxsx≥−1,\inner{u\otimes w}{(\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z+\sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x)\,u\otimes w} =r_zs_z+r_xs_x\ge-1 ,⟨u⊗w,(σz​⊗σz​+σx​⊗σx​)u⊗w⟩=rz​sz​+rx​sx​≥−1, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the vectors (rz,rx)(r_z,r_x)(rz​,rx​) and (sz,sx)(s_z,s_x)(sz​,sx​), whose lengths are at most 111. The mean in an ensemble is Tr⁡(ρA)\Tr(\rho A)Tr(ρA), by (3.6), and for an ensemble of products this is the weighted average of the members’ means. So it is also at least −1-1−1. In ψ−\psi^-ψ− the sum is −2-2−2.

So correlation along one axis is not entanglement. Neither is entanglement a property of a basis. The vectors ∣01⟩\ket{01}∣01⟩, t0=12(∣01⟩+∣10⟩)t_0=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01}+\ket{10})t0​=2​1​(∣01⟩+∣10⟩) and ψ−\psi^-ψ− are all eigenvectors of σz⊗σz\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_zσz​⊗σz​ with the eigenvalue −1-1−1. The first is a product, and the other two are entangled.

10.5 The two spins of hydrogen

In a hydrogen atom the electron’s spin and the proton’s spin interact through their magnetic moments. Take the electron in its lowest orbital, keep it there, and apply no magnetic field. Then only the two spins can change. They form two qubits, and, once the constant energy of the orbital is dropped, their effective Hamiltonian is H=ℏν4 σ⋅σ,σ⋅σ=σx⊗σx+σy⊗σy+σz⊗σz,\begin{equation} \label{eq:10:hf} H=\frac{\hbar\freq}4\,\sigvec\cdot\sigvec,\qquad \sigvec\cdot\sigvec=\sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x+\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y +\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z , \end{equation}H=4ℏν​σ⋅σ,σ⋅σ=σx​⊗σx​+σy​⊗σy​+σz​⊗σz​,(10.3) where ν>0\freq>0ν>0 is an angular frequency, measured as ν/2π=1 420.405 751 768\freq/2\pi=1\,420.405\,751\,768ν/2π=1420.405751768 MHz. To find its levels we use the swap WWW on C2⊗C2\C^2\otimes\C^2C2⊗C2, the matrix with W(u⊗w)=w⊗uW(u\otimes w)=w\otimes uW(u⊗w)=w⊗u.

10.6 Hydrogen in a magnetic field

A magnetic field of magnitude BBB along zzz acts on each spin by its own Larmor term (Lecture 1). Summary

  • Two systems: the state space is the tensor product, and a reading of one system is a reading of A⊗11A\otimes\IdA⊗11 (Postulate 10.1).
  • The rank test: a state is a product exactly when its coefficient matrix has rank 111 (Theorem 10.4).
  • Hydrogen’s two spins: their ground state ψ−\psi^-ψ− is entangled, and their interaction entangles a product state (Example 10.6).
  • In a field: the ground state tends to a product as the field grows (Example 10.8).
