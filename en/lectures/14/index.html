Lecture 14

Identical particles: bosons and fermions

What this lecture does

Two electrons are particles of the same kind, and no experiment tells which of them is the first. This lecture finds what that does to the states of a pair. They are symmetric or antisymmetric under the swap of the two particles, and which one is a law of nature: antisymmetric for electrons, symmetric for photons. For electrons it gives Pauli’s exclusion. An orbital holds at most two electrons, as Lecture 8 promised. For photons it gives an interference effect. Two photons, alike in everything but their input port, that meet at a balanced beam splitter one at each input always leave by the same output. On two sites, hopping and repulsion favour the singlet of two electrons, with no magnetic force.

14.1 Exchange

Let two particles of the same kind each have the state space V=CnV=\C^nV=Cn. By Postulate 10.1 the pair has the state space V⊗VV\otimes VV⊗V. The first factor belongs to “particle 111” and the second to “particle 222”. For particles of the same kind these labels are only the slots of the tensor product. Every reading that can be made on the pair treats the two particles alike.

The swap WWW of Lecture 10, W(u⊗w)=w⊗uW(u\otimes w)=w\otimes uW(u⊗w)=w⊗u, exchanges the slots. It permutes the orthonormal basis ei⊗eje_i\otimes e_jei​⊗ej​, so it is unitary and self-adjoint, and W2=11W^2=\IdW2=11. Its eigenvalues are ±1\pm1±1, and its two eigenspaces are the spaces of Linear Algebra, Def. 12.20: Sym2V={ψ:Wψ=ψ},Λ2V={ψ:Wψ=−ψ}.\mathrm{Sym}^2V=\{\psi:W\psi=\psi\},\qquad \Lambda^2V=\{\psi:W\psi=-\psi\} .Sym2V={ψ:Wψ=ψ},Λ2V={ψ:Wψ=−ψ}. For an orthonormal basis uiu_iui​ of VVV, the vectors ui⊗uiu_i\otimes u_iui​⊗ui​ and 12(ui⊗uj+uj⊗ui)\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(u_i\otimes u_j+u_j\otimes u_i)2​1​(ui​⊗uj​+uj​⊗ui​), i<ji<ji<j, are an orthonormal basis of Sym2V\mathrm{Sym}^2VSym2V, and the vectors 12(ui⊗uj−uj⊗ui)\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(u_i\otimes u_j-u_j\otimes u_i)2​1​(ui​⊗uj​−uj​⊗ui​), i<ji<ji<j, of Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V.

Two particles are identical if no reading tells them apart. We take this to mean that an observable AAA of the pair satisfies WAW=AWAW=AWAW=A, that is, [A,W]=0\comm AW=0[A,W]=0. This is a requirement on which matrices count as readings of the pair, not a theorem. The observable B⊗11+11⊗BB\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes BB⊗11+11⊗B, the sum of one reading BBB over both particles, satisfies it, since W(B⊗11)W=11⊗BW(B\otimes\Id)W=\Id\otimes BW(B⊗11)W=11⊗B. The observable B⊗11B\otimes\IdB⊗11, a reading of “the first particle”, does not satisfy it, unless BBB is a multiple of 11\Id11.

Proposition 14.1 (Exchange sectors). Let AAA be an observable and HHH a constant Hamiltonian of the pair, both commuting with WWW. A reading of AAA that starts in Sym2V\mathrm{Sym}^2VSym2V leaves the pair in Sym2V\mathrm{Sym}^2VSym2V, for every reading of positive probability, and the evolution Ut=e−itH/ℏU_t=e^{-\ii tH/\hbar}Ut​=e−itH/ℏ maps Sym2V\mathrm{Sym}^2VSym2V into itself. The same holds for Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V.

Proof. By Lemma 4.3, every projection EaE_aEa​ of AAA commutes with WWW. If Wψ=ψW\psi=\psiWψ=ψ, then WEaψ=EaWψ=EaψWE_a\psi=E_aW\psi=E_a\psiWEa​ψ=Ea​Wψ=Ea​ψ, so Lüders’ state EaψE_a\psiEa​ψ is symmetric. The swap WWW is a symmetry of HHH (Definition 6.3), so it commutes with every UtU_tUt​, and WUtψ=UtψWU_t\psi=U_t\psiWUt​ψ=Ut​ψ in the same way. For Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V, replace ψ\psiψ by −ψ-\psi−ψ on the right. 

So whether the pair is in Sym2V\mathrm{Sym}^2VSym2V or in Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V is a property that no reading and no evolution of identical particles changes. Moreover, by Proposition 6.4, the swap is a conserved observable, with the readings ±1\pm1±1.

14.2 Bosons and fermions

Which sector? Experiment answers, and the answer depends only on the kind of particle.

Postulate 14.2 (Symmetrisation). The states of two identical particles lie in Sym2V\mathrm{Sym}^2VSym2V for some kinds of particle, called bosons, and in Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V for the others, called fermions. Here VVV is the state space of one particle with all its degrees of freedom, its motion and its spin. For NNN identical particles the state is symmetric, for bosons, or antisymmetric, for fermions, under the swap of every two of them.

Photons are bosons. Electrons, protons and neutrons are fermions. The spin–statistics theorem of relativistic quantum field theory, proved by Fierz in 1939 and Pauli in 1940, says that particles of integer spin are bosons and particles of half-integer spin are fermions. We cite it. It does not follow from the finite theory of this course, and here the postulate carries it.

14.3 Pauli’s exclusion

Theorem 14.3 (Pauli’s exclusion). For every u∈Vu\in Vu∈V, the vector u⊗uu\otimes uu⊗u lies in Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V only if u=0u=0u=0. So two identical fermions are never both in the same one-particle state.

Proof. Since W(u⊗u)=u⊗uW(u\otimes u)=u\otimes uW(u⊗u)=u⊗u, a vector u⊗uu\otimes uu⊗u in Λ2V\Lambda^2VΛ2V has u⊗u=−u⊗uu\otimes u=-u\otimes uu⊗u=−u⊗u, so u⊗u=0u\otimes u=0u⊗u=0. By the inner product of §10.1, its norm is ∥u∥2\norm u^2∥u∥2, so u=0u=0u=0. 

An electron has a motion and a spin, so its state space is V=O⊗C2V=O\otimes\C^2V=O⊗C2: an orbital space OOO, for example the NNN sites of the ring of Lecture 8, times the spin. To use the spin states of Lecture 13, group the factors of the pair by kind. The map RRR from V⊗V=(O⊗C2)⊗(O⊗C2)V\otimes V=(O\otimes\C^2)\otimes(O\otimes\C^2)V⊗V=(O⊗C2)⊗(O⊗C2) to (O⊗O)⊗(C2⊗C2)(O\otimes O)\otimes(\C^2\otimes\C^2)(O⊗O)⊗(C2⊗C2) with R((o⊗s)⊗(o′⊗s′))=(o⊗o′)⊗(s⊗s′)R\bigl((o\otimes s)\otimes(o'\otimes s')\bigr)=(o\otimes o')\otimes(s\otimes s')R((o⊗s)⊗(o′⊗s′))=(o⊗o′)⊗(s⊗s′) on products of basis vectors takes an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis, so it is unitary. Since WOW_OWO​ and WSW_SWS​ commute and each has the eigenvalues ±1\pm1±1, the antisymmetric states of two electrons are, after RRR, (Sym2O⊗Λ2C2)⊕(Λ2O⊗Sym2C2).\begin{equation} \label{eq:14:split} \bigl(\mathrm{Sym}^2O\otimes\Lambda^2\C^2\bigr)\oplus \bigl(\Lambda^2O\otimes\mathrm{Sym}^2\C^2\bigr) . \end{equation}(Sym2O⊗Λ2C2)⊕(Λ2O⊗Sym2C2).(14.1) Every antisymmetric state is the sum of a vector of the first subspace and a vector of the second. Corollary 14.4 (Two electrons to an orbital). Two electrons both in the orbital u∈Ou\in Ou∈O, that is, in a state R†((u⊗u)⊗χ)\adj R\bigl((u\otimes u)\otimes\chi\bigr)R†((u⊗u)⊗χ), have the spin state χ\chiχ on the line of the singlet ψ−\psi^-ψ−. Three electrons cannot all be in one orbital.

So an orbital holds at most two electrons, and two electrons in one orbital have opposite spins, in the singlet. §14.6 fills the orbitals of benzene.

14.4 Two particles at a beam splitter

A beam splitter is a half-silvered mirror with two input ports and two output ports. In the ideal two-mode model a particle’s state space is C2\C^2C2, with the basis e1e_1e1​, e2e_2e2​: the particle is in port 111 or port 222. Every other degree of freedom, the polarisation of a photon or the shape and the arrival time of its wave packet, is the same for both particles, so its factor is one fixed vector and is left out. The splitter acts on each particle by one unitary UUU on C2\C^2C2, and so on the pair by U⊗UU\otimes UU⊗U. It is balanced if ∣Uij∣2=12\abs{U_{ij}}^2=\tfrac12∣Uij​∣2=21​ for all i,ji,ji,j, so that a single particle leaves by either output with probability 12\tfrac1221​.

Two detectors, one at each output, record a coincidence when each fires, that is, when there is one particle at each output. Its projection is Ec=E1⊗E2+E2⊗E1,Ei=∣ei⟩ ⁣⟨ei∣,E_c=E_1\otimes E_2+E_2\otimes E_1,\qquad E_i=\proj{e_i} ,Ec​=E1​⊗E2​+E2​⊗E1​,Ei​=∣ei​⟩⟨ei​∣, which commutes with WWW. One particle enters at each input.

14.5 Exchange includes spin

Electrons are fermions, so one might expect two electrons at a beam splitter always to leave apart. That is true only for some spin states.

Example 14.6 (Two electrons at a beam splitter). Each electron has V=C2⊗C2V=\C^2\otimes\C^2V=C2⊗C2, port times spin, and the splitter acts on the port only, by U⊗11U\otimes\IdU⊗11 on each electron. One electron enters at each input, and all degrees of freedom other than port and spin are in the same fixed one-particle state. We take states that are a product of a port part and a spin part. By (14.1), the port part is symmetric when the spin part is the singlet, and antisymmetric when it is a triplet state. The coincidence projection acts on the ports only.

  1. Spins in the singlet. The port part is 12(e1⊗e2+e2⊗e1)\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(e_1\otimes e_2+e_2\otimes e_1)2​1​(e1​⊗e2​+e2​⊗e1​), and by Theorem 14.5 a balanced splitter gives coincidence probability 000, as for bosons.

  2. Both spins up. The spin part ∣↑↑⟩\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}∣↑↑⟩ is symmetric, so the port part is 12(e1⊗e2−e2⊗e1)\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(e_1\otimes e_2-e_2\otimes e_1)2​1​(e1​⊗e2​−e2​⊗e1​), and the coincidence probability is 111.

The antisymmetry is a property of the whole state, port and spin together, not of each part. Two electrons can bunch like photons, if their spins are in the singlet. The zero also needs the assumptions of the model. If the two photons differ in polarisation or arrival time, the factor left out is not the same for both, and the zero is lost (Exercise Sheet 14).

14.6 Many fermions: Slater determinants

By Postulate 14.2, the states of NNN identical fermions lie in ΛNV\Lambda^NVΛNV, the vectors of V⊗⋯⊗VV\otimes\cdots\otimes VV⊗⋯⊗V (NNN factors) that change sign under the swap of any two factors. For εU≫κ\varepsilon_U\gg\kappaεU​≫κ the splitting is about 4κ2/εU4\kappa^2/\varepsilon_U4κ2/εU​. The spins prefer to be opposite, although HHH has no term that acts on the spins. In the triplet the orbital part is antisymmetric, so the electrons never share a site and cannot lower their energy by hopping. This is antiferromagnetic exchange, from hopping, repulsion and exclusion, not from magnetic moments as is hydrogen’s σ⋅σ\sigvec\cdot\sigvecσ⋅σ. With several orbitals on a site the triplet can be lower (Hund’s rule). For εU>0\varepsilon_U>0εU​>0 the lowest state is not a single Slater determinant (Exercise Sheet 14).

Hong, Ou and Mandel sent pairs of photons from a nonlinear crystal into the two inputs of a beam splitter in 1987, and delayed one photon by a variable time. When the two wave packets overlapped in time at the splitter, the coincidences dropped nearly to zero, the dip that now carries their names. Away from the overlap the photons are told apart by their arrival times, and the coincidences return to their background level (probability 12\tfrac1221​ in the ideal balanced model). The width of the dip, less than a picosecond, measured the length of the photons’ wave packets. Pauli proposed his exclusion principle in 1925, to explain the shells of electrons in atoms, before the antisymmetric states that express it were known. In the hydrogen molecule the two electrons form a bond in the singlet, close to the covalent state of §14.7 (Heitler and London, 1927, with orbitals that are not orthogonal). Anderson (1959) explained the antiferromagnetism of many insulators by the exchange of §14.7.

Summary

  • Exchange sectors: the symmetric and antisymmetric states are kept by every reading and evolution of identical particles (Proposition 14.1).
  • Pauli’s exclusion: no two fermions share a one-particle state; an orbital holds at most two electrons (Theorem 14.3, Corollary 14.4).
  • Two particles at a beam splitter: in the ideal two-mode model, one particle at each input of a balanced splitter, bosons never leave apart and fermions always do (Theorem 14.5).
  • Exchange: on two sites, hopping, repulsion and exclusion put the singlet below the triplet (Proposition 14.8).
