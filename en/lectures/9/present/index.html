Lecture 9

Position and momentum on the ring: the Weyl pair

A particle on the ring of Lecture 8 can be read in two ways: by its site, or by its Fourier mode. The two readings play the roles of position and momentum. One unitary, the discrete Fourier transform, exchanges them. No state gives a certain outcome in both readings. More precisely, the numbers of sites and of modes that a state occupies multiply to at least NNN. Two unitaries label the two readings, and they satisfy Weyl’s relation DT=ζTDDT=\zeta TDDT=ζTD. Their products are a basis of all matrices, and for a ring of prime size, readings in N+1N+1N+1 bases fix any state. No finite matrices satisfy the canonical relation [Q,P]=iℏ11[Q,P]=\ii\hbar\Id[Q,P]=iℏ11 of position and momentum on a line, as a matrix identity. This is where the finite theory stops.

9.2 The clock and the shift

Each reading has a unitary whose eigenvectors are its basis. For the modes this is the shift TTT of Lecture 6, Tex=ex+1Te_x=e_{x+1}Tex​=ex+1​, with Tfk=ζ−kfkTf_k=\zeta^{-k}f_kTfk​=ζ−kfk​ (§8.2). The clock is a function of any observable whose projections are those of the site reading, D=∑xζx∣ex⟩ ⁣⟨ex∣D=\sum_x\zeta^x\proj{e_x}D=∑x​ζx∣ex​⟩⟨ex​∣, and the shift is a function of any observable whose projections are those of the mode reading. If two such observables commuted, so would DDD and TTT. So by Theorem 5.2 the site and mode readings are not compatible.

Neither DDD nor TTT is an observable. For N≥3N\ge3N≥3 they are unitary and not self-adjoint, since some of their eigenvalues, such as ζ\zetaζ, are not real. Their eigenvalues only label the readings. We do not take a logarithm to turn them into observables.

9.5 Where the finite theory stops

For a particle on a line, position and momentum are observables QQQ and PPP that satisfy, on a suitable set of states that the next course specifies, QP−PQ=iℏ11.QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\Id .QP−PQ=iℏ11. The clock and the shift look like exponentials of such a pair. On the line, the unitaries e−iaP/ℏe^{-\ii aP/\hbar}e−iaP/ℏ translate by aaa, as TTT translates by one site, and eibQ/ℏe^{\ii bQ/\hbar}eibQ/ℏ multiply by a phase that grows with the position, as DDD does. They satisfy e−iaP/ℏeibQ/ℏ=e−iab/ℏeibQ/ℏe−iaP/ℏ,e^{-\ii aP/\hbar}e^{\ii bQ/\hbar}=e^{-\ii ab/\hbar}e^{\ii bQ/\hbar}e^{-\ii aP/\hbar} ,e−iaP/ℏeibQ/ℏ=e−iab/ℏeibQ/ℏe−iaP/ℏ, the form of Weyl’s relation that the next course proves. The finite relation DT=ζTDDT=\zeta TDDT=ζTD holds for matrices on CN\C^NCN. The relation for the generators QQQ and PPP does not, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 9.8 (No finite position and momentum). There are no n×nn\times nn×n matrices QQQ and PPP, for any n≥1n\ge1n≥1, with QP−PQ=iℏ11QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\IdQP−PQ=iℏ11.

Proof. By the cyclicity of the trace, Tr⁡(QP−PQ)=Tr⁡(QP)−Tr⁡(PQ)=0\Tr(QP-PQ)=\Tr(QP)-\Tr(PQ)=0Tr(QP−PQ)=Tr(QP)−Tr(PQ)=0, while Tr⁡(iℏ11)=iℏn≠0\Tr(\ii\hbar\Id)=\ii\hbar n\ne0Tr(iℏ11)=iℏn=0. 

This is Linear Algebra, Prop. 14.24. The canonical relation needs operators on a space of infinite dimension, and that is where the next course begins. It starts with a particle on a line, and with the uncertainty relation ΔQ ΔP≥ℏ/2\Delta Q\,\Delta P\ge\hbar/2ΔQΔP≥ℏ/2, which has no finite form here.

Weyl wrote down the clock and the shift in 1927, as a finite model of position and momentum, and Schwinger proved Theorem 9.6 in 1960. Wootters and Fields showed in 1989 that readings in mutually unbiased bases determine a state with the fewest kinds of reading, and that such complete sets of N+1N+1N+1 bases exist when NNN is a power of a prime. For N=6N=6N=6 no complete set is known. The same pair appears in signal processing, where a signal sampled at NNN equally spaced times is transformed by FFF into NNN discrete frequency components. There Theorem 9.3 says that a short pulse has a broad spectrum. Donoho and Stark proved it in this form in 1989, and used it to recover signals with missing samples.

For prime NNN, N+1N+1N+1 readings determine ρ\rhoρ (Proposition 9.7). Limit: no matrices satisfy QP−PQ=iℏ11QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\IdQP−PQ=iℏ11 (Proposition 9.8).

Summary

  • The Weyl pair: the clock and the shift label the site and mode readings, satisfy DT=ζTDDT=\zeta TDDT=ζTD, and are exchanged by the discrete Fourier transform (Theorem 9.2).
  • Support uncertainty: no state is sharp in both readings (Theorem 9.3).
  • A basis of clocks and shifts: the N2N^2N2 matrices DrTsD^rT^sDrTs are an orthogonal basis, and for prime NNN the site reading and the eigenbases of the DuTD^uTDuT determine every state (Theorem 9.6, Proposition 9.7).
  • No finite position and momentum: QP−PQ=iℏ11QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\IdQP−PQ=iℏ11 has no matrix solution (Proposition 9.8).
