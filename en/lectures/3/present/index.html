Lecture 3

Mixtures and the Bloch ball

Atoms straight from an oven read +1+1+1 with probability 12\tfrac1221​ along every axis. No state of Lecture 1 does that. Such a beam is a random mixture of states. This lecture shows that everything a measurement can reveal about a mixture is held in one matrix, the density matrix, and that measurements reveal all of it. For a qubit, the density matrices fill the ball inside the Bloch sphere.

3.1 An unpolarised beam

Send the silver atoms straight from the oven into a Stern–Gerlach magnet, with no magnet before it. Whatever the direction m\mvecm of the magnet, half of the atoms read +1+1+1 and half read −1-1−1. A beam with this property is called unpolarised.

No state of Lecture 1 behaves like this. By Corollary 1.8, the state with Bloch vector r\blochvr reads +1+1+1 along m=r\mvec=\blochvm=r with certainty. So the atoms from the oven are not all in one state.

A natural guess is that each atom leaves the oven in some state, and that the state varies at random from atom to atom. Suppose, for instance, that the oven prepares ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ or ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩, each with probability 12\tfrac1221​. Along an axis m\mvecm, the atoms prepared ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ read +1+1+1 with probability 12(1+mz)\tfrac12(1+m_z)21​(1+mz​), and those prepared ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ with probability 12(1−mz)\tfrac12(1-m_z)21​(1−mz​). In total, 12⋅12(1+mz)+12⋅12(1−mz)=12for every m,\frac12\cdot\frac12(1+m_z)+\frac12\cdot\frac12(1-m_z)=\frac12 \quad\text{for every }\mvec,21​⋅21​(1+mz​)+21​⋅21​(1−mz​)=21​for every m, as observed. But an oven that prepares ∣+x⟩\ket{+x}∣+x⟩ or ∣−x⟩\ket{-x}∣−x⟩, each with probability 12\tfrac1221​, gives 12\tfrac1221​ for every m\mvecm just as well. Which guess is right? This lecture shows that no measurement can tell.

In an oven at several hundred degrees, silver atoms collide with the walls and with each other at random. Nothing in the oven picks out a direction in space. A sample of water in an NMR magnet, left in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings at the temperature TTT, is nearly unpolarised too. Statistical physics then weights the two energy states of a proton in proportion to e−E/kTe^{-E/kT}e−E/kT, where kkk is Boltzmann’s constant. In a field of 1 T1\ \mathrm T1 T at 300 K300\ \mathrm K300 K this gives a Bloch vector of length tanh⁡(ℏγB/2kT)≈3.4⋅10−6\tanh(\hbar\gamma B/2kT)\approx3.4\cdot10^{-6}tanh(ℏγB/2kT)≈3.4⋅10−6. The NMR signal of Lecture 2 comes from turning this small excess into the xyxyxy-plane with a π/2\pi/2π/2-pulse.

3.2 Random preparations

The guess needs only finite probability theory, which we now recall. A random experiment has finitely many outcomes kkk, with probabilities pk>0p_k>0pk​>0 that sum to 111. Outcomes that cannot occur are left out. The conditional probability Prob⁡(b∣k)\Prob(b\mid k)Prob(b∣k) is the probability of an event bbb when kkk is known to have occurred. The law of total probability is Prob⁡(b)=∑kpk Prob⁡(b∣k).\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:total} \Prob(b)=\sum_kp_k\,\Prob(b\mid k). \end{equation}Prob(b)=k∑​pk​Prob(b∣k).(3.1) When Prob⁡(b)>0\Prob(b)>0Prob(b)>0, Bayes’ rule gives the probability of kkk once bbb is known: Prob⁡(k∣b)=pk Prob⁡(b∣k)Prob⁡(b).\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:bayes} \Prob(k\mid b)=\frac{p_k\,\Prob(b\mid k)}{\Prob(b)} . \end{equation}Prob(k∣b)=Prob(b)pk​Prob(b∣k)​.(3.2) The probability of two events in turn is the product of the probability of the first and the conditional probability of the second.

Definition 3.1 (Ensemble). An ensemble is a finite list {(pk,ψk)}\{(p_k,\psi_k)\}{(pk​,ψk​)} of probabilities pk>0p_k>0pk​>0 with ∑kpk=1\sum_kp_k=1∑k​pk​=1 and unit vectors ψk∈C2\psi_k\in\C^2ψk​∈C2. A single state [ψ][\psi][ψ] is the ensemble with one member, {(1,ψ)}\{(1,\psi)\}{(1,ψ)}. The definitions and the branch calculation of §3.3 work unchanged in Cn\C^nCn. Lecture 4 uses them there.

Remark 3.5 (Conditioning changes the weights). After a branch, the ensemble has the weights qkq_kqk​ of Bayes’ rule, not the old weights pkp_kpk​. On a qubit the error does not show in a single reading along an axis, since every member that can give the reading is left in the same pure state. In Cn\C^nCn it shows (Lecture 4).

Proposition 3.7 (The statistics determine the density matrix). For every density matrix ρ\rhoρ of a qubit and each a∈{x,y,z}a\in\{x,y,z\}a∈{x,y,z}, ra=2Prob⁡(+1 along ea)−1.r_a=2\Prob(+1\text{ along }\mathbf e_a)-1 .ra​=2Prob(+1 along ea​)−1. So two density matrices that give the same probabilities along ex\mathbf e_xex​, ey\mathbf e_yey​ and ez\mathbf e_zez​ are equal.

Proof. By the rules above, Prob⁡(+1 along ea)=12(1+ra)\Prob(+1\text{ along }\mathbf e_a)=\tfrac12(1+r_a)Prob(+1 along ea​)=21​(1+ra​). So the three probabilities fix r\blochvr. By Theorem 3.6, r\blochvr fixes ρ=12(11+r⋅σ)\rho=\tfrac12(\Id+\blochv\cdot\sigvec)ρ=21​(11+r⋅σ). 

Together with Theorem 3.4, this shows that two ensembles of qubits give the same probabilities in every experiment if and only if they have the same density matrix. The three probabilities are measured on three separate groups of systems, all prepared alike. One system cannot serve for all three, since a reading along xxx replaces its state by ±ex\pm\mathbf e_x±ex​. The measured fractions only estimate the probabilities. Exercise Sheet 3 shows what can go wrong.

3.6 What a mixture does not say

The beam from the oven has r=0\blochv=0r=0 and ρ=1211\rho=\tfrac12\Idρ=21​11. The equal mixture of ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ and ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ has this density matrix, and so has the equal mixture of ∣+x⟩\ket{+x}∣+x⟩ and ∣−x⟩\ket{-x}∣−x⟩, or of the two states at ±m\pm\mvec±m for any axis m\mvecm. By Theorem 3.4, no experiment on the atoms tells these preparations apart. This answers the question of §3.1. A density matrix does not record which states were mixed, and nothing measured on the atoms does either.

A mixture is also not a superposition. The state ∣+x⟩=12(∣↑⟩+∣↓⟩)\ket{+x}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket\uparrow+\ket\downarrow)∣+x⟩=2​1​(∣↑⟩+∣↓⟩) and the mixture 1211\tfrac12\Id21​11 of ∣↑⟩\ket\uparrow∣↑⟩ and ∣↓⟩\ket\downarrow∣↓⟩ give the same statistics along zzz. Along xxx, the state ∣+x⟩\ket{+x}∣+x⟩ reads +1+1+1 with certainty, and the mixture only with probability 12\tfrac1221​.

In this course every density matrix comes from an ensemble, or, in Lecture 11, from one half of a pair of systems. States of a particle, which need infinitely many dimensions, are not covered. Quantum Mechanics III: Foundations asks what a state is in general, and finds the density matrices again.

Summary

  • Statistics depend on the density matrix only: a branch has probability Tr⁡(KρK†)\Tr(K\rho\adj K)Tr(KρK†) and, if this is positive, leaves KρK†K\rho\adj KKρK†, normalised (Theorem 3.4).
  • The Bloch ball: the density matrices of a qubit are the points of the unit ball, and the pure ones its surface (Theorem 3.6).
  • The statistics determine the density matrix: the probabilities along the xxx, yyy and zzz axes fix r\blochvr (Proposition 3.7).
