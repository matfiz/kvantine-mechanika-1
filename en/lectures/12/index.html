Lecture 12

Bell’s theorem: no local hidden variables

What this lecture does

The electron and the proton of hydrogen’s ground state ψ−\psi^-ψ− give opposite readings along every common axis. Could each reading have been fixed in advance, by some property of the atom that quantum mechanics does not describe? This lecture states what such a local hidden-variable model is, and proves the CHSH inequality. In every such model, a certain combination SSS of four correlations satisfies ∣S∣≤2\abs S\le2∣S∣≤2. The singlet gives ∣S∣=22\abs S=2\sqrt2∣S∣=22​. No local model reproduces the predictions of quantum mechanics, and experiments agree with quantum mechanics. Three spins give a sharper conflict, with certainties in place of averages.

The two readings are equal with probability 12(1+E)=12(1−a⋅b)\tfrac12(1+E)=\tfrac12(1-\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)21​(1+E)=21​(1−a⋅b). Along a common axis, b=a\mathbf b=\mathbf ab=a, they are always opposite, since E(a,a)=−1E(\mathbf a,\mathbf a)=-1E(a,a)=−1. Lecture 10 found this along zzz and xxx. It holds along every axis.

Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen drew a conclusion from such perfect correlations in 1935, in a different example. Bohm put it in terms of two spins. A reading of the proton along a\mathbf aa predicts with certainty what a reading of the electron along a\mathbf aa will give. They added a premise: a reading made on the proton, far away, does not disturb the electron’s physical situation. From the two they concluded that the electron’s reading along a\mathbf aa must have been fixed before it was made, for every axis a\mathbf aa, by something the state ψ−\psi^-ψ− does not record. Their premise is stronger than Proposition 11.8, which says only that the electron’s pooled statistics do not change. Bell asked in 1964 whether such a description can reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics. This lecture shows that it cannot.

12.2 Local models

Two observers, one at each spin, each choose one of two settings: an axis ax\mathbf a_xax​, x=1,2x=1,2x=1,2, for the electron, and an axis by\mathbf b_yby​, y=1,2y=1,2y=1,2, for the proton. Each records a reading, α=±1\alpha=\pm1α=±1 and β=±1\beta=\pm1β=±1. A model of the experiment that fixes the readings in advance, or at least their probabilities, has the following form.

Definition 12.2 (Local model). A local model consists of

  1. a finite set Λ\LambdaΛ of hidden states λ\lambdaλ, with probabilities p(λ)≥0p(\lambda)\ge0p(λ)≥0, ∑λp(λ)=1\sum_\lambda p(\lambda)=1∑λ​p(λ)=1, that do not depend on the settings xxx and yyy;

  2. for each λ\lambdaλ and each setting xxx, probabilities P1(α∣x,λ)P_1(\alpha\mid x,\lambda)P1​(α∣x,λ) of the electron’s readings α=±1\alpha=\pm1α=±1, and for each λ\lambdaλ and each setting yyy, probabilities P2(β∣y,λ)P_2(\beta\mid y,\lambda)P2​(β∣y,λ) of the proton’s readings β=±1\beta=\pm1β=±1;

such that the readings α\alphaα and β\betaβ occur together, at the settings xxx and yyy, with probability Prob⁡(α,β∣x,y)=∑λ∈Λp(λ) P1(α∣x,λ) P2(β∣y,λ).\begin{equation} \label{eq:12:local} \Prob(\alpha,\beta\mid x,y)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(\lambda)\, P_1(\alpha\mid x,\lambda)\,P_2(\beta\mid y,\lambda) . \end{equation}Prob(α,β∣x,y)=λ∈Λ∑​p(λ)P1​(α∣x,λ)P2​(β∣y,λ).(12.2)

Each hypothesis has a meaning. The hidden state λ\lambdaλ is prepared with the pair, before the settings are chosen. That its probabilities do not depend on the settings is an assumption. Given λ\lambdaλ, the electron’s reading depends on the electron’s setting only, and not on the proton’s setting or reading. This is locality. The two readings can be made far apart, at the same time. The model may still correlate the readings, through λ\lambdaλ.

In a deterministic local model every P1(α∣x,λ)P_1(\alpha\mid x,\lambda)P1​(α∣x,λ) and P2(β∣y,λ)P_2(\beta\mid y,\lambda)P2​(β∣y,λ) is 000 or 111. Each reading is then fixed by λ\lambdaλ and the setting. This is the case “the readings were there all along”. The definition allows more, and the theorem below covers all of it.

For each λ\lambdaλ put the local means Aˉx(λ)=P1(+1∣x,λ)−P1(−1∣x,λ),Bˉy(λ)=P2(+1∣y,λ)−P2(−1∣y,λ),\bar A_x(\lambda)=P_1(+1\mid x,\lambda)-P_1(-1\mid x,\lambda),\qquad \bar B_y(\lambda)=P_2(+1\mid y,\lambda)-P_2(-1\mid y,\lambda) ,Aˉx​(λ)=P1​(+1∣x,λ)−P1​(−1∣x,λ),Bˉy​(λ)=P2​(+1∣y,λ)−P2​(−1∣y,λ), numbers between −1-1−1 and 111. By (12.2), the correlation at the settings xxx, yyy is Exy=∑α,βαβ Prob⁡(α,β∣x,y)=∑λp(λ)(∑ααP1(α∣x,λ))(∑ββP2(β∣y,λ))=∑λp(λ) Aˉx(λ)Bˉy(λ).\begin{equation} \label{eq:12:Exy} \begin{split} E_{xy}&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha\beta\,\Prob(\alpha,\beta\mid x,y) =\sum_\lambda p(\lambda)\Bigl(\sum_\alpha\alpha P_1(\alpha\mid x,\lambda)\Bigr) \Bigl(\sum_\beta\beta P_2(\beta\mid y,\lambda)\Bigr)\\ &=\sum_\lambda p(\lambda)\,\bar A_x(\lambda)\bar B_y(\lambda) . \end{split} \end{equation}Exy​​=α,β∑​αβProb(α,β∣x,y)=λ∑​p(λ)(α∑​αP1​(α∣x,λ))(β∑​βP2​(β∣y,λ))=λ∑​p(λ)Aˉx​(λ)Bˉy​(λ).​(12.3)

12.3 The CHSH inequality

Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt (CHSH) combined the four correlations into S=E11+E12+E21−E22.\begin{equation} \label{eq:12:S} S=E_{11}+E_{12}+E_{21}-E_{22} . \end{equation}S=E11​+E12​+E21​−E22​.(12.4)

Theorem 12.4 (CHSH). In every local model, ∣S∣≤2\abs S\le2∣S∣≤2.

Proof. First, for real numbers uuu and vvv, ∣u+v∣+∣u−v∣=2max⁡(∣u∣,∣v∣).\abs{u+v}+\abs{u-v}=2\max(\abs u,\abs v) .∣u+v∣+∣u−v∣=2max(∣u∣,∣v∣). The case ∣v∣≥∣u∣\abs v\ge\abs u∣v∣≥∣u∣ is the same with uuu and vvv exchanged. Next, fix λ\lambdaλ, and put u=Bˉ1(λ)u=\bar B_1(\lambda)u=Bˉ1​(λ), v=Bˉ2(λ)v=\bar B_2(\lambda)v=Bˉ2​(λ). 12.4 The singlet violates it

By Theorem 12.4, no local model gives these four correlations. This is Bell’s theorem, in the form of Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt: no local model reproduces the predictions of quantum mechanics for the singlet. The perfect anticorrelation of §12.1 alone does not decide the question. A local model can give E(a,a)=−1E(\mathbf a,\mathbf a)=-1E(a,a)=−1 for every axis, as Exercise Sheet 12 shows. It is the correlations at the angles in between that no local model can match.

Entanglement is needed for this.

12.5 Three spins: certainty instead of statistics

The CHSH inequality compares averages. With three spins the conflict is sharper. It concerns predictions that quantum mechanics makes with certainty. By Postulate 10.1, applied twice, three spins have the state space C2⊗C2⊗C2\C^2\otimes\C^2\otimes\C^2C2⊗C2⊗C2, with the basis ∣jkl⟩=∣j⟩⊗∣k⟩⊗∣l⟩\ket{jkl}=\ket j\otimes\ket k\otimes\ket l∣jkl⟩=∣j⟩⊗∣k⟩⊗∣l⟩, and a reading of the second spin alone is a reading of 11⊗A⊗11\Id\otimes A\otimes\Id11⊗A⊗11. Lemma 12.8 (Certainty forces values). If in a local model of three spins the product of the three readings at some settings has the mean ±1\pm1±1, then for every λ\lambdaλ with p(λ)>0p(\lambda)>0p(λ)>0 the three local means at these settings are ±1\pm1±1.

Proof. A mean of numbers between −1-1−1 and 111, with weights p(λ)p(\lambda)p(λ), is ±1\pm1±1 only if every number with positive weight is ±1\pm1±1. A product of three numbers between −1-1−1 and 111 has modulus 111 only if each of them has. 

Perfect correlations force values. For each such λ\lambdaλ the readings at these settings are fixed, as Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued.

12.6 What the violation does and does not say

The violation allows no signalling. Whatever axis is chosen for the electron, and as long as nobody conditions on its outcome, the proton reads +1+1+1 along every axis with probability Tr⁡(ρ2G+)=12\Tr(\rho_2G_+)=\tfrac12Tr(ρ2​G+​)=21​, by Proposition 11.8 and ρ2=1211\rho_2=\tfrac12\Idρ2​=21​11. The choice of setting on one side cannot be read off the other side’s readings alone. The correlations show only when the two records are brought together. “Nonlocal” in this sense means that no local model exists. It does not mean an effect that travels faster than light.

The theorem has three hypotheses, and an experiment must justify each. First, the factorised law of Definition 12.2: given λ\lambdaλ, the reading on one side depends on neither the setting nor the reading on the other side. Experiments choose each setting at a time and place from which no signal at the speed of light could reach the other side’s reading in time. This rules out such signals. It does not by itself prove the factorised law. Second, every run must give a reading ±1\pm1±1 and be counted. If many runs are lost, the counted ones need not obey the inequality. Third, the settings must be independent of the hidden state, even when the hidden state is prepared earlier. No experiment can prove this, and it remains an assumption.

Quantum mechanics does not give every value of SSS either. For every state of two qubits and every four settings, ∣S∣≤22\abs S\le2\sqrt2∣S∣≤22​ (Tsirelson’s bound, Exercise Sheet 12). The singlet with the settings of Example 12.5 reaches it.

The first tests were made with pairs of photons, whose polarisation is a qubit. Freedman and Clauser (1972) measured the polarisation correlations of photons emitted in pairs by calcium atoms and found them in agreement with quantum mechanics and in violation of a Bell inequality, under auxiliary assumptions. Aspect, Dalibard and Roger (1982) switched the analysers’ settings while the photons were in flight. A polariser at the angle ϑ\varthetaϑ reads the polarisation along a Bloch axis at the angle 2ϑ2\vartheta2ϑ, so the Bloch angles of Example 12.5, 45∘45^\circ45∘ apart, are polariser angles 22.5∘22.5^\circ22.5∘ apart (Lecture 5). In 2015 three groups closed the loopholes of locality and of lost runs in one experiment each: Hensen and co-workers with electron spins in diamond, using the CHSH form, and Giustina and co-workers and Shalm and co-workers with photons, using a related inequality of Clauser and Horne. Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2022 for such experiments. Pan, Bouwmeester, Daniell, Weinfurter and Zeilinger (2000) prepared three photons in the GHZ state and found the correlations of §12.5, imperfect as every count is, in agreement with quantum mechanics.
