Lecture 4

Many levels: the postulates in Cn\C^nCn

What this lecture does

Many systems have more than two readings. Their states are the rays of Cn\C^nCn. This lecture carries the four postulates and the density matrix from C2\C^2C2 to Cn\C^nCn, and finds two things that a qubit could not show. First, an eigenvalue may have an eigenspace larger than a line, and a reading of it then keeps the whole part of the state inside that eigenspace. Next, under a constant Hamiltonian with many energy levels, a mean can oscillate only at the differences of the levels divided by ℏ\hbarℏ, the Bohr frequencies. The lecture also shows that, between measurements, the same probabilities and means follow if the observables change in time and the states do not.

4.1 More than two readings

A Stern–Gerlach magnet splits a beam of silver atoms into two. Beams of some other atoms split into three or more, and Lecture 7 finds which numbers can occur. The energy of an atom has many possible readings too. We need systems with more than two readings.

In the theory of Lecture 1, an observable is a self-adjoint matrix and its readings are its eigenvalues. By the spectral theorem, a self-adjoint matrix with nnn distinct eigenvalues has nnn orthonormal eigenvectors, so it acts on a space of dimension at least nnn. A system whose states are the rays of Cn\C^nCn is called an nnn-level system. The qubit is the case n=2n=2n=2.

Our example in this part of the course has n=3n=3n=3. It has an orthonormal basis e+e_+e+​, e0e_0e0​, e−e_-e−​, which we keep in this order, and the Hamiltonian H=ε(∣e+⟩ ⁣⟨e+∣+∣e−⟩ ⁣⟨e−∣)=(ε0000000ε),ε>0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:4:HT} H=\varepsilon\bigl(\proj{e_+}+\proj{e_-}\bigr) =\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \varepsilon>0 . \end{equation}H=ε(∣e+​⟩⟨e+​∣+∣e−​⟩⟨e−​∣)=​ε00​000​00ε​​,ε>0.(4.1) Its energies are 000, read in the state e0e_0e0​, and ε\varepsilonε, read in every state of the plane spanned by e+e_+e+​ and e−e_-e−​. Lecture 7 explains the labels +++, 000, −-−. The box The physics below describes a system with this Hamiltonian.

The nitrogen–vacancy (NV) centre is a defect in diamond: a nitrogen atom in place of a carbon atom, next to a site from which a carbon atom is missing. In its negatively charged form, NV−^-−, the defect has two unpaired electrons. 4.2 The postulates in Cn\C^nCn

Postulate 4.1 (A system of nnn levels).

  1. States. The states are described by unit vectors ψ∈Cn\psi\in\C^nψ∈Cn, and two unit vectors describe the same state if and only if they differ by a phase. A state is a ray [ψ][\psi][ψ].

  2. Observables. An observable is a self-adjoint n×nn\times nn×n matrix A=∑aa EaA=\sum_aa\,E_aA=∑a​aEa​, where aaa runs over the distinct eigenvalues and EaE_aEa​ is the orthogonal projection onto the whole eigenspace of aaa. Its possible readings are its eigenvalues.

  3. Measurement. An ideal measurement of AAA on [ψ][\psi][ψ] reads aaa with probability Prob⁡(a)=⟨ψ,Eaψ⟩=∥Eaψ∥2\Prob(a)=\inner\psi{E_a\psi}=\norm{E_a\psi}^2Prob(a)=⟨ψ,Ea​ψ⟩=∥Ea​ψ∥2 (Born’s rule). If the reading is aaa, which requires Prob⁡(a)>0\Prob(a)>0Prob(a)>0, the state immediately afterwards is the ray [Eaψ∥Eaψ∥]\Bigl[\frac{E_a\psi}{\norm{E_a\psi}}\Bigr][∥Ea​ψ∥Ea​ψ​] (Lüders’ rule).

  4. Evolution. Between measurements the state vector obeys iℏ dψtdt=Hψt,\ii\hbar\,\frac{\dd\psi_t}{\dd t}=H\psi_t ,iℏdtdψt​​=Hψt​, where the Hamiltonian HHH is the observable whose readings are the energies.

For n=2n=2n=2 these are the postulates of Lecture 1. The Hamiltonian may depend on time, as in Lecture 2. In this lecture it is constant. The definitions of an ensemble and of its density matrix did not use n=2n=2n=2 either, and neither did the theorem that the statistics depend on the density matrix only (Theorem 3.4). So for a density matrix ρ\rhoρ on Cn\C^nCn, a reading aaa has probability Tr⁡(ρEa)\Tr(\rho E_a)Tr(ρEa​) and, if this is positive, leaves the density matrix EaρEaTr⁡(ρEa).\frac{E_a\rho E_a}{\Tr(\rho E_a)} .Tr(ρEa​)Ea​ρEa​​.

Remark 4.2 (The space of states). Linear Algebra, Ex. 10.36, previews the formula (4.3). What the theorem adds is (4.4). The energies themselves never appear in a mean. Only their differences do, through the angular frequencies εk−εlℏ,\frac{\varepsilon_k-\varepsilon_l}{\hbar} ,ℏεk​−εl​​, the Bohr frequencies of the system. They include 000, for k=lk=lk=l, and come in pairs of opposite sign. A mean can oscillate only at these frequencies, which are the same for every observable and every state. The observable and the state decide how much each frequency contributes, through the numbers Tr⁡(EkρElA)\Tr(E_k\rho E_lA)Tr(Ek​ρEl​A), and some may contribute nothing. For example, a stationary state, or the observable 11\Id11, gives a constant mean. A qubit with energies c±12ℏν0c\pm\tfrac12\hbar\freq_0c±21​ℏν0​ has the single positive Bohr frequency ν0\freq_0ν0​. In Lecture 2, ν0\freq_0ν0​ was the frequency of the resonance. Atoms absorb and emit light at their Bohr frequencies. This is Bohr’s rule for spectral lines, named here and not derived.

Three warnings follow from the theorem. First, every nonzero vector of a degenerate eigenspace is an eigenvector of HHH, so every state of that eigenspace is stationary. For (4.1), the states e+e_+e+​, e−e_-e−​ and χ+=12(e++e−)\chi_+=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(e_++e_-)χ+​=2​1​(e+​+e−​) are all stationary. So the stationary pure states do not form a basis fixed by HHH alone. Second, the energy statistics of every state are constant, and yet a state may change in time. §4.6 shows one. Third, the formula (4.3) needs HHH constant. A Hamiltonian H(t)H(t)H(t) that changes in time has its own evolution U(t,s)U(t,s)U(t,s), as in Lecture 2.

In other words, a degenerate reading keeps the cross terms inside its eigenspace, and a finer reading that is recorded only in part loses them. Lüders’ rule picks no vector in the eigenspace. It keeps the whole projection of the state. The two experiments agree on every observable that commutes with AAA. Only an observable that does not commute with AAA can tell them apart. §4.6 gives one.

Summary

  • Evolution by the energy levels: under a constant Hamiltonian, the energy statistics are constant, every mean is a sum of terms oscillating at the Bohr frequencies, and a state is stationary if and only if its density matrix commutes with HHH (Theorem 4.4).
  • Observables in time: for a constant Hamiltonian, At=Ut†AUtA_t=\adj{U_t}AU_tAt​=Ut†​AUt​ gives the same statistics between measurements, and At=AA_t=AAt​=A for all ttt exactly when [A,H]=0\comm AH=0[A,H]=0 (Proposition 4.5).
  • A degenerate reading is not a reading half recorded: both give the same probabilities, but the degenerate reading keeps the cross terms inside its eigenspace (Proposition 4.6).
