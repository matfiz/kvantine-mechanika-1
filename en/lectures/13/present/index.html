Lecture 13

Adding angular momenta

Two spins rotate together. The total angular momentum of a pair is the sum of the two, and it is again an angular momentum in the sense of Lecture 7. So the pair splits into multiplets, and the counting of Lecture 7 tells which. Two multiplets of spins j1j_1j1​ and j2j_2j2​ give one multiplet of each spin from ∣j1−j2∣\abs{j_1-j_2}∣j1​−j2​∣ to j1+j2j_1+j_2j1​+j2​. Two spins one half make spin 111, the triplet, and spin 000, the singlet ψ−\psi^-ψ− of hydrogen’s ground state. For a≠0a\ne0a=0, an interaction of the form a J(1)⋅J(2)a\,\mathbf J^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf J^{(2)}aJ(1)⋅J(2) has one level for each total spin. Inside one multiplet every vector operator acts as a multiple of J\mathbf JJ, and this gives the splitting of an atomic level in a weak magnetic field.

13.6 Vector operators and the Zeeman effect

In its first excited level, 3p3p3p, the outer electron of sodium has an orbital angular momentum L\mathbf LL of spin 111 in the sense of Lecture 7 (the next course explains its origin) and its spin S\mathbf SS. The yellow light of sodium lamps is two lines, at 589.0 and 589.6 nm in air. Both come from the outer electron of the sodium atom falling from its first excited level, 3p3p3p, to its lowest level, 3s3s3s (§13.6). The line at 589.0 nm starts from j=32j=\tfrac32j=23​ and the line at 589.6 nm from j=12j=\tfrac12j=21​. Zeeman saw spectral lines split in a magnetic field in 1896. The sodium lines split into more components than the three of the “normal” effect, and this “anomalous” Zeeman effect was understood only after the electron’s spin was proposed, by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit in 1925.
